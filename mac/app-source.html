How to read the map & make sense of the degrees

One root, everywhere

Every root is labeled 1, regardless of octave. Change the root to transpose the whole pattern. The guitar’s tuning stays E–A–D–G–B–e; only each note’s relationship to the root changes.

Minor doesn’t restart the numbers

Degrees always refer to the major scale on the selected root. Minor therefore uses 1, 2, ♭3, 4, 5, ♭6, ♭7. A ♭ lowers a degree one half step; a ♯ raises it one half step. These labels are not finger numbers.

Chords: a recipe, then a voicing

Select Chords to map the tones of a chord on every string. The formula is its recipe; the notes you choose across the strings make a voicing. This map does not require you to play every highlighted note. 9, 11 and 13 are the same pitch classes as 2, 4 and 6, labeled by their role in the chord. A double flat (♭♭) lowers a degree two half steps.

A mode needs a home note

The chosen root is the home note. D Dorian shares notes with C major, but D should sound like home. Try returning to a root at the end of each phrase.

How the colors work

The strongest accent marks the root. A soft accent marks unaltered degrees in your selection; a contrasting warm color marks altered degrees. Dashed notes are outside your selection. The notes-only switch hides them. Each theme preserves these roles.

Intervals & spelling

1 = root; ♭2/2 = minor/major second; ♭3/3 = minor/major third; 4 = perfect fourth; ♯4/♭5 = tritone; 5 = perfect fifth; ♭6/6 = minor/major sixth; ♭7/7 = minor/major seventh. W = whole step (2 frets); H = half step (1 fret); 3H = 3 frets.

Some scales need names such as E♯ (the same pitch as F) or F♯♯ (the same pitch as G). The spelling preserves the scale degree. An outside-scale note uses a simple degree spelling; its label can change when it becomes part of another scale.

How key changes and chord labels work

Changing the key root transposes your sequence. Changing major/minor starts fresh; Undo restores it. The chooser’s color setting affects new chords only. Every “Inside the key” choice is checked against the selected scale; a simpler chord fills in when an extension does not fit.

Labels are practical interpretations of the chord’s notes and the following one or two chords. “Possible” means the resolution is not confirmed. They update when you edit or reorder. With Loop on, the last chord also considers the start of the sequence. A melody or different voicing can suggest another interpretation.

Borrowing, substitutions & reading the symbols

One key is the reference

All root degrees use the major scale on the key root as a reference, even in minor: ♭III, ♭VI and ♭VII make the lowered roots explicit. Roman uppercase means a major triad; lowercase means minor; ° means diminished; ø7 means half-diminished. A plain 7 means a minor seventh above that chord’s root; maj7 means a major seventh. Number mode spells out m, dim and m7♭5 instead of using letter case.

A temporary destination

V7/ii means “the dominant seventh of ii.” The slash points to a target chord; it is not a bass-note instruction. In C major, A7 → Dm briefly pulls toward ii without requiring a new home key. These suggested destinations are options, not rules.

Parallel is not relative

Parallel keys share a tonic (C major and C minor). Relative keys share a key signature (C major and A minor). Borrowing a chord from the parallel key adds color without necessarily changing the home key. In minor, V7 and vii°7 use the raised seventh from harmonic minor and appear under “Pull toward home.”

Dominant alternatives

A tritone substitute replaces V7 with a dominant seventh whose root is a half step above the destination: D♭7 → C can replace G7 → C. Their thirds and sevenths share the same two pitches, spelled differently. vii°7 is another leading-tone approach. Try smooth half-step moves between voices and check the melody against any borrowed notes.
